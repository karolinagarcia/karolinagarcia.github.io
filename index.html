About Me

I'm an astrophysicist studying how galaxies form and evolve, with a particular focus on connecting the small-scale physics of the interstellar medium to the large-scale structure of the Universe.

I'm currently a CAPS Survey Science Fellow at the National Center for Supercomputing Applications (NCSA), University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. I earned my PhD in Astronomy from the University of Florida, after transitioning from a career in engineering and industry.

My research combines cosmological hydrodynamical simulations, radiative transfer, and machine learning to predict the emission of atomic and molecular gas across cosmic time. I lead the development of SLICK, a framework connecting the physical conditions of gas in simulated galaxies to observable emission lines and line-intensity mapping experiments.

Beyond research, I'm passionate about making science accessible through public talks, educational projects, and social media. Outside astronomy, I enjoy music, singing, playing guitar, and sports.

Research Projects

SLICK: Connecting Interstellar Medium Physics to Cosmological Observations

How can we predict what galaxies emit across cosmic time without losing the complexity of the gas within them?

I developed SLICK, a computational framework combining cosmological hydrodynamical simulations with models of the interstellar medium, radiative transfer, and machine learning to predict molecular and atomic line emission.

The ongoing SLICK-2 framework extends this approach to model CO, [CI], and [CII] emission across cosmological volumes while retaining the diversity of physical conditions within galaxies.

Read the original SLICK paper

Predicting Line-Intensity Mapping Observations

Line-intensity mapping measures the collective emission from galaxies across large cosmological volumes, including galaxies too faint to be detected individually.

I use SLICK to predict emission-line luminosity functions, CO excitation ladders, and intensity mapping power spectra across cosmic time. These predictions help connect the physics of galaxy formation to observations from current and future line-intensity mapping experiments.

AGN and the Interstellar Medium

Active galactic nuclei can dramatically alter the physical conditions of gas in galaxies. I'm investigating how AGN radiation affects atomic and molecular line emission, and how these effects propagate into predictions for galaxy surveys and line-intensity mapping.

Finding Galaxy Clusters in the Boötes Field

I lead the development and characterization of a galaxy cluster catalog in the Boötes field using deep multiwavelength observations and photometric redshift information.

The catalog contains 825 galaxy cluster and group candidates spanning redshifts 0.08 to 2.0, including 147 candidates at z ≥ 1.5.

We characterize the catalog's purity, completeness, and photometric redshift performance, enabling studies of galaxy clusters across cosmic time.

Measuring the Three-Point Correlation Function of Galaxies

The three-point correlation function provides information about the non-Gaussian structure of the Universe beyond what can be measured using two-point statistics.

We developed improved, symmetric line-of-sight estimators for the anisotropic three-point correlation function, with applications to large-scale galaxy surveys.

Read the paper

Cosmology with Type Ia Supernovae

During my master's research, I investigated how Type Ia supernova peculiar velocities can constrain the growth of cosmic structure.

We explored how survey area, depth, and observational strategies affect constraints on cosmological parameters such as σ8.
