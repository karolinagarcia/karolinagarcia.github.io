SLICK: Connecting the Physics of Galaxies to the Observable Universe

Galaxy formation • Interstellar medium physics • Cosmological simulations • Emission-line modeling

Galaxies are complex systems. Their gas exists in different physical phases, exposed to radiation, turbulence, and a variety of environmental conditions. These processes determine how galaxies form stars, evolve, and emit radiation.

But how do we connect this detailed physics to the light we observe from galaxies across the Universe?

This is the central motivation behind SLICK, a modeling framework I developed to predict atomic and molecular line emission from cosmological simulations.

The Scientific Challenge

Cosmological simulations follow the formation and evolution of galaxies across enormous volumes of the Universe. However, many of the processes responsible for their observable emission occur on scales much smaller than these simulations can resolve.

As a result, connecting simulated galaxies to observations requires models that bridge the gap between small-scale interstellar medium physics and cosmological structure.

SLICK was designed to address this challenge by incorporating physically motivated models of the gas and its emission into large-scale simulations.

Our Approach

Rather than assigning line luminosities to galaxies using only empirical scaling relations, SLICK predicts emission from the physical conditions of their gas.

The framework combines cosmological hydrodynamical simulations with models of the multiphase interstellar medium, thermal and chemical processes, and radiative transfer.

This allows us to explore how variations in gas density, metallicity, radiation fields, and other physical conditions influence the emission of different atomic and molecular lines.

The central idea: Emission-line properties emerge from the modeled physics of the gas, rather than being prescribed solely by galaxy-wide empirical relations.

What Can We Learn?

By connecting interstellar medium physics to observable line emission, SLICK enables investigations of several questions:

  • How do molecular and atomic gas tracers evolve across cosmic time?
  • What can emission-line ratios tell us about the physical conditions inside galaxies?
  • How do changes in galaxy formation physics affect observable line emission?
  • What signals should we expect from upcoming line-intensity mapping surveys?

Beyond the Original SLICK: SLICK-2

The next stage of this work extends the original framework toward a more comprehensive treatment of atomic and molecular emission.

SLICK-2 brings together models of multiple gas phases and emission lines, enabling a more unified investigation of the interstellar medium in cosmological simulations.

This development is particularly important for connecting galaxy formation models to multi-line observations and for interpreting line-intensity mapping measurements.

SLICK-2 methodology paper: Manuscript in preparation.
