SLICK applications · Line-intensity mapping

Predicting the Line-Emitting Universe

Line-intensity mapping measures the combined emission from galaxies and diffuse sources across large cosmic volumes—even when individual galaxies are too faint to detect.

Rather than resolving one galaxy at a time, line-intensity mapping (LIM) uses fluctuations in spectral-line brightness to probe how matter and emitting gas are distributed in three dimensions. Different lines, including CO and [C II], provide complementary windows into star formation and the interstellar medium.

Why LIM Matters

LIM offers access to large volumes and faint source populations that are difficult to characterize with conventional galaxy surveys. Measurements of mean line intensity, clustering, and cross-correlations with galaxy catalogs can help connect the cosmic distribution of matter to the evolution of emitting gas.

Projects such as COMAP, TIME, TIM, and FYST/Prime-Cam illustrate the range of observational approaches and target lines in this growing field. Their observing strategies, status, and science goals differ; the links below lead to project information.

The Astrophysical Modeling Challenge

Interpreting an intensity map requires more than knowing the underlying dark-matter distribution. The measured signal also depends on which galaxies emit, how bright they are in a given line, the scatter in luminosity at fixed galaxy properties, and how emission varies across environments and cosmic time.

Simple empirical relations are useful starting points, but they can hide the dependence of emission on gas conditions and feedback. This is especially limiting when comparing several lines or trying to infer astrophysics from a measured power spectrum.

What SLICK Contributes

Physics-informed line luminosities

SLICK computes line emission from modeled gas conditions, creating a route to investigate why a luminosity relation or line ratio changes rather than assuming a fixed galaxy-wide prescription.

Multi-tracer predictions

The SLICK-2 extensions aim to model multiple molecular, neutral, and ionized tracers consistently, enabling studies of complementary lines and their potential cross-correlations.

From galaxies to intensity statistics

Applying SLICK to simulated galaxy populations provides a path toward constructing line-intensity maps, luminosity functions, and clustering predictions, including power spectra and cross-correlations.

More informative inference

In future analyses, varying the underlying gas and galaxy-formation physics could help quantify modeling uncertainty and identify which combinations of lines are most informative about astrophysical processes.

Current Direction

My ongoing SLICK-LIM research focuses on connecting emission predictions to cosmological observables and examining how assumptions about galaxy formation and the ISM affect LIM forecasts. These are research goals and ongoing developments, not claims that every analysis described here has already been completed.
